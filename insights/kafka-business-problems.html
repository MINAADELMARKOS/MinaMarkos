Understanding Kafka Through Real Business Problems

Choose event streaming by the decision deadline, replay requirement, and cost of coupling—not by message volume alone.

By Mina Adel Markos · Published 17 November 2026 · 3 min read

Begin with the time between an event and a decision

A telecom usage event is useful for billing tomorrow and for fraud detection now. A nightly export can satisfy the first requirement while making the second impossible. Before choosing Kafka, write down who needs the event, the latest useful arrival time, and what happens if a consumer is unavailable. Those three answers define the service you need more precisely than “real time.”

In banking, an authorization request belongs on a bounded request-response path with an explicit timeout and a system of record. Publishing the resulting authorization event lets risk analytics, notifications, and reconciliation evolve independently. Kafka can distribute that fact; it should not silently become the authority for the account balance. The application still needs rules for concurrent updates, deduplication, and reconciliation.

Scheduled batch versus an event log

A scheduled batch pipeline asks what changed since its last watermark. It is economical when decisions tolerate delay and the source supports consistent extraction. It needs overlap handling, late-arrival rules, and a way to replay a failed interval. Streaming replaces the schedule with continuously advancing positions in an event log. It lowers waiting time, but introduces always-running consumers, lag monitoring, schema compatibility, and retention planning.

Consider an analytics team that needs yesterday’s revenue before 08:00. A small, reliable batch job may be a better investment than a streaming platform. Now add customer notifications within a minute and three independently deployed consumers. An append-only log becomes more attractive because each consumer can advance at its own rate. The comparison is operating cost against an actual latency requirement, not old technology against new technology.

What Kafka supplies—and what remains your job

Kafka provides a replicated partitioned log, consumer positions, retention, and independent consumption. The producer can continue while a reporting consumer is offline, provided the outage does not exhaust storage or outlast retention. A replay can rebuild a view from retained records without asking the source database to reproduce every historical change.

Kafka does not validate that an amount is correct, grant business-level idempotency, guarantee global ordering across partitions, or fix an unavailable downstream API. A malformed event can be replayed perfectly and remain wrong. Add event identifiers, schema evolution rules, ownership, and source-to-sink reconciliation. Treat those as part of the architecture, not paperwork after launch.

A practical adoption decision

For the telecom example, define a freshness SLO such as a stated percentage of eligible events becoming available to the fraud feature pipeline within a chosen deadline. Separate event time from ingestion time so delayed source delivery is visible. Track oldest unprocessed event age, duplicate outcomes, recovery time, and cost per million useful events.

Pilot one event type with a deliberate consumer outage and a replay. Measure catch-up capacity while new traffic continues. If arrival rate is 1,000 records/s and sustainable consumption is 1,200 records/s, only 200 records/s are available to clear backlog. A 720,000-record backlog therefore needs about an hour, assuming stable rates. That recovery budget may change the capacity decision more than steady-state latency.
